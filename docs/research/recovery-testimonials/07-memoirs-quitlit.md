# 07 — Memoirs, quit-lit and long-form journalism

First-person accounts of behaviour change from published memoirists, method-book authors and
journalists. Collected for the recovery-support product.

**Status: complete.** ~90 verified short quotes across **25 authors**, and **40 named techniques and
reframes** (T-1 to T-40), organised in four lanes: journalism/essay, literary memoir, addiction
science, and method books.

**Read first:** §1 (what is and isn't verified), §2 (copyright), §3 + §10 + §14 (the disagreement
map). §14 ends with the five convergences that hold *across* mutually hostile traditions — those are
the safest findings in the document.

## 1. What could and could not be sourced

### Sourcing method

Every quoted string in this document was **fetched from a live URL during research**. Nothing is
quoted from memory. Where a passage is known to exist but no fetchable copy was found, it is
recorded under **UNVERIFIED** in prose, with no quotation marks.

Two verification tiers are used:

- **[RAW]** — the exact string was confirmed present in the raw HTML of the page (downloaded and
  grepped character-for-character). Highest confidence.
- **[FETCHED]** — the string came back from a page fetch, and where practical was re-checked by a
  second verification pass against the same URL.

This distinction matters. During research, a page-fetch returned an apparent Holly Whitaker quote
("Know what you can't fuck with…") that **did not exist** in the raw HTML of that page. It was
discarded. Fetch summarisation can fabricate plausible-sounding quotes, so every load-bearing quote
here was re-checked.

### Paywalled or blocked — could not source

- **The Atlantic** — blocked to the crawler. Gabrielle Glaser's "The Irrationality of Alcoholics
  Anonymous" (April 2015) could not be fetched. Recorded as UNVERIFIED below.
- **The Guardian** — blocked to the crawler. No Guardian long-form could be used.
- **The New York Times** — blocked. Holly Whitaker's op-ed was recovered only via a third-party
  page that reproduces it verbatim (noted inline).
- **Elise Loehnen / Substack interview with Whitaker** — paid-subscriber paywall; only the opening
  sentence was visible, and only that sentence is quoted.
- **Edith Zimmerman, "My First Year Sober"** (Spiralbound/Medium, 21 Feb 2018) — the piece is an
  **image-based comic**; no machine-readable text. Known to exist, not quotable. Notable only

### A caution about Goodreads

Goodreads quote pages are user-transcribed and **unreliable**. The Goodreads page for Ann Dowsett
Johnston's *Drink* attributes to her the verse "I love a martini—but two at the most. Three I'm
under the table, four, I'm under the host." That is **Dorothy Parker**, quoted inside the book, not
Dowsett Johnston's own writing. Goodreads-only quotes in this document are marked as such and
should be corroborated before any product use.

## 2. Copyright note — READ BEFORE ANY PRODUCT USE

Every work cited here is an **in-copyright commercial book, essay or article**. Quotes in this
document are deliberately held to one or two sentences, for the purpose of criticism, review and
research (fair dealing / fair use).

**For product use:**

- Keep quotes to one or two sentences.
- Always attribute: author, title, publisher/outlet, year.
- Do not chain several short quotes into a long passage — that reconstitutes the original and
  defeats the fair-use argument.
- Anything longer than a sentence or two, or any use of a quote as decorative/marketing copy rather
  than as commentary, **needs permission from the rightsholder**.
- Publisher-authorised excerpts (e.g. the McKowen excerpt below) are cleared for reading at their
  host site — that is not the same as a licence to republish.

Twelve axes, spread across three sections. **§3** holds the three that matter most for product

**The two most consequential for the product are Axis J** (is there an end state?) **and Axis I**
(does relapse reset the count?) — both bear directly on streak mechanics.

### Axis A — Is "alcoholic" a useful identity or a harmful label?

| Position | Who | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| The label is the doorway; naming it is what makes change possible | **Marian Keyes**, AA-rooted memoirists | "I'm just an ordinary alcoholic, trying my best to stay sober one day at a time." |
| Self-conferred, at any severity — don't wait to be certified | **Sarah Hepola** | "Alcoholism is a self-diagnosis." |
| The binary label is what *causes* late diagnosis | **Annie Grace** | it makes people choose between "self-diagnosing a fatal and incurable illness or admitting to being weak-willed" |
| The label is a stigmatising misdirection — the substance is the problem, not the minority who can't handle it | **Holly Whitaker** | "we've created a separate disease called alcoholism and forced it upon the minority of the population who are willing to admit they can't control their drinking" |
| Refuses to adjudicate — replaces diagnosis with self-inquiry | **Ruby Warrington** | "I need to state up front that I am not a doctor, a brain scientist, or an addiction expert, and so it's really not for me to diagnose your personal drinking habits." |

Note the **question-swap** that Whitaker and Warrington share: both replace *"am I an alcoholic?"*
with *"is this doing anything for me?"* — see Technique T-3.

### Axis B — Does AA work?

- **Against:** Whitaker (patriarchal, built by and for upper-middle-class white men in the 1930s);
  Glaser in *The Atlantic* (UNVERIFIED — see below).
- **Empirically, for:** the 2020 Cochrane review (Kelly, Humphreys & Ferri) is the strongest
  counterweight to the Glaser/Whitaker case and post-dates Glaser's article by five years.
  Cochrane's own summary reports "42 % of participants participating in AA would remain completely
  abstinent one year later, compared to 35% of participants receiving other treatments", and that
  "four of the five economics studies we identified showed considerable cost-saving benefits for AA
  and related 12-step clinical programs".
  [FETCHED] — https://www.cochrane.org/about-us/news/new-cochrane-review-finds-alcoholics-anonymous-and-12-step-facilitation-programs-help-people (10 March 2020)
- **Sideways:** Kristi Coulter neither joins nor rejects — she takes the goal seriously and the
  reverence not at all (see T-6).

**Product implication:** this axis is genuinely unresolved and is the single most contested question
in the corpus. The product should not adopt a house position on AA.

### Axis C — Forever vs. right now

- **McKowen:** commitment to *forever* is the thing that defeats people; the unit of commitment must
  be the present moment (T-1).
- **Coulter:** the opposite emphasis — she describes a year of trying to *want* to stop, which
  failed, and a stop that worked only when she simply stopped (T-5).

These are less contradictory than they look: both reject *wanting* and *promising* as the
mechanism. Neither treats motivation as the lever.

## 5. Techniques and reframes — journalism and essay lane

### T-1 — Shrink the unit of commitment ("forget forever")
**Source:** Laura McKowen, *We Are the Luckiest*.
**What you literally do:** Stop making any promise about the future. Replace the lifetime vow with a
single-instance one — *I will not drink right now, no matter what* — and re-issue it each moment.
**Why it works (her account):** *forever* is the thing that generates the panic, and the panic is
what drives the drink. Removing the time horizon removes the load.
**Support:** "A lifetime of sobriety was impossible, but a moment of sobriety was not." [RAW]
**Echoes:** the AA "one day at a time" formula; McKowen's version is finer-grained (moment, not day).

### T-2 — Drop the public declaration
**Source:** McKowen.
**What you literally do:** Deliberately *do not* announce the decision — no pronouncement, no
texts, no telling anyone.
**Notable because it contradicts** the standard commitment-device advice (tell people, make it
costly to fail). McKowen's account is that going public re-imports *forever*.
**Support:** "I didn't make any grand pronouncements; I didn't declare my intentions to anyone." [RAW]

### T-3 — The question-swap: from diagnosis to cost-benefit
**Source:** Holly Whitaker; independently Ruby Warrington.
**What you literally do:** Refuse the question *"am I an alcoholic?"* and substitute *"is this
actually doing anything for me?"* / *"would life be better without it?"*
**Why it works:** the diagnostic question is answerable only by self-accusation, so it invites
denial and stalls indefinitely. The cost-benefit question is answerable from ordinary evidence and
does not require accepting a stigmatised identity first.
**Support:** Whitaker — "For years, I was asking myself whether or not I was an alcoholic versus
really asking myself whether or not alcohol was actually providing any benefit to me."
Warrington — "Would life be better without alcohol?"
**Strongest candidate in this lane for direct product use.** It is a single swappable question, it
is stated almost identically by two independent authors, and it front-loads no identity claim.

### T-4 — Borrow a future self ("a lantern")
**Source:** Kristi Coulter.
**What you literally do:** Read, daily, the accounts of people six to twelve months further along —
close enough to be credible, far enough to show the road. Coulter did an hour a night.
**Support:** "I spent at least an hour a night reading posts from people who were far enough along
in their sobriety to serve as a lantern for me." [FETCHED]
**Note the dose:** an hour a night in early abstinence. This is a substantial time commitment, not
a light touch.

### T-5 — Stop trying to *want* to stop
**Source:** Kristi Coulter.
**What this is:** an explicit **negative finding**. Coulter spent a year attempting to generate the
desire to quit, treating motivation as the prerequisite. It failed. What worked was acting without
the desire being resolved.
**Support:** "I tried so hard to want to not drink; I went on like that for a year." followed by
"In the end, the way I stopped was by stopping." [FETCHED, verified]
**Converges with T-1:** both authors remove *feeling ready* from the causal path.

### T-6 — Serious but not reverent
**Source:** Kristi Coulter.
**What you literally do:** Hold the goal as non-negotiable while refusing the solemn register,
the jargon and the piety that recovery culture attaches to it. Coulter used irreverent sober
podcasts and humour as the delivery vehicle.
**Why it matters:** she names the *fear of earnestness* as what kept her out — she thought sobriety
would "crowd out humor, and that was not an appealing prospect."
**Support:** "I realized that I could take my sobriety seriously, but not reverently." [FETCHED, verified]
**Product implication:** tone is a retention variable, not decoration. A solemn product loses the
people who are repelled by solemnity.

### T-7 — Treat capacity as trainable, not given
**Source:** Kristi Coulter.
**What you literally do:** Expect early sobriety to be hard *in the way untrained running is hard*,
and read the difficulty as a training signal rather than evidence of unsuitability.
**Support:** "You have to build the muscle. If you start running and expect it to be easy, you'll be
disappointed right away." [FETCHED, verified]

### T-8 — Interoceptive onset: let the body ask first
**Source:** Ruby Warrington.
**What you literally do:** Attend to the hungover morning as *data* — the specific physical and
mental state — before trying to reach a verdict about your drinking.
**Support:** "This inquiry began as a conversation with my body before the words fully crystallized
in my mind." [RAW]
**Contrast:** where Whitaker's swap (T-3) is cognitive, Warrington's entry point is somatic. Both
arrive at the same question.

## 9. Techniques and reframes — memoir and science lanes

### T-9 — Catch the conditional-postponement script
**Source:** Caroline Knapp.
**What you literally do:** Watch for the sentence form *"I'll cut down when X gets better"* and treat
it as a symptom, not a plan — because X is permanently one step away.
**Support:** "That was my favorite line: I'll drink less when things get better."
**Echoes:** Whitaker's and Warrington's question-swap (T-3) attacks the same stalling structure from
a different angle.

### T-10 — Model it as a breakup, not a battle
**Source:** Caroline Knapp.
**How the reframe works:** Casting the substance as a *lover* rather than an enemy licenses grief,
nostalgia and mourning as legitimate stages, so longing stops being read as failure. A person who
expects to grieve does not interpret missing it as relapse-in-progress.
**Support:** "It happened this way: I fell in love and then, because the love was ruining everything I cared about, I had to fall out."
**Disagrees with:** Hepola's infrastructure frame (T-12) — see Axis H.

### T-11 — Action coaxes belief (do it before you mean it)
**Source:** Mary Karr; independently Leslie Jamison.
**What you literally do:** Perform the behaviour — the meeting, the prayer, the next right thing —
*without* waiting to believe in it, and let conviction be the downstream product rather than the
entry requirement.
**Support:** Karr — "Faith is not a feeling, she says. It's a set of actions." [RAW]
Jamison — "Action could coax belief, rather than testifying to it." [RAW]
**Why it matters:** two authors with opposite temperaments converge on it independently, and it
directly dissolves the "I'd be a hypocrite" objection.
**Echoes:** Coulter's T-5 (stop trying to *want* it) — three authors, same structural move: remove
sincerity from the critical path.

### T-12 — Separate the physical act from the identity vacuum
**Source:** Sarah Hepola. **The single most actionable insight in this document.**
**How the reframe works:** Abstaining is trivial at the muscular level. All the difficulty is the
collapse of the scaffolding the substance was holding up — how you talk to people, who you are at a
party, how intimacy starts, how you work.
**Therefore:** effort spent on *more resistance* is misallocated. Effort should go to building
replacement scaffolding.
**Support:** "Not taking a drink was easy. Just a matter of muscle movement… The impossible part was everything else."
**Product implication:** a product that only counts abstinence is instrumenting the easy half.

### T-13 — Self-diagnosis as the entry gate
**Source:** Sarah Hepola.
**What you literally do:** Confer the label on yourself rather than waiting for a clinician, a
crisis, or a rock bottom to certify you. This makes the decision available at *any* point on the
severity curve.
**Support:** "Alcoholism is a self-diagnosis."
**Note the tension:** this makes the identity label *self-serve*, which partly answers Whitaker's
objection (Axis A) without discarding the label. Compare Szalavitz's T-19.

### T-14 — Become a beginner on purpose
**Source:** Sarah Hepola.
**What you literally do:** Adopt explicit learner status in the sober version of ordinary activities
instead of expecting your competence to carry over. She pairs it with giving herself permission to
fail without that threatening her identity.
**Support:** "The lesson of sobriety is that I had to become a beginner."
**Echoes:** Coulter's T-7 ("build the muscle") — same expectation-setting move.

### T-15 — Expect the relief phase to expire
**Source:** Leslie Jamison. **A negative finding about a first-line technique.**
**What this is:** The recognition-relief of hearing others describe the same compulsion is real and
powerful — *and* it wore off for her within a few months, replaced by irritation at clichés and
jargon, which she names as partly her "deep-seated desire to drink… looking for ways out."
**Why it matters:** a person whose support-group relief fades will read that as proof it isn't
working. Naming the phase in advance defuses it.
**Support:** "The sense of relief… was absolute." then "It was a few months afterward that I started to get irritated…"

### T-16 — Give the withdrawal curve a published shape
**Source:** Anna Lembke.
**What you literally do:** Before starting an abstinence trial, tell the person the timeline: worst
at days 10–14 (anxiety, irritability, insomnia, dysphoria, cravings), reward re-sensitisation from
around week 4.
**Why it works:** the timeline converts "this isn't working" into "this is day 9". The forecast is
the intervention.
**Support:** the four-weeks and 10-to-14-days quotes above.

### T-17 — Self-binding: build the barrier while you're calm
**Source:** Anna Lembke.
**What you literally do:** At a non-craving moment, insert a barrier so the decision isn't made in
the craving state — phone out of the bedroom, delete the app, lockbox, a rule about days or
categories.
**Support:** "Self binding strategies are a way of putting both a literal and a metacognitive barrier between ourselves and our drug of choice…"
**Caveat:** the familiar three-part taxonomy (physical / chronological / categorical) appears in
secondary summaries of the book, **not** in any source fetched here. Describe it in your own words
or source the book — see §10.

### T-18 — Stop escalating consequences
**Source:** Maia Szalavitz.
**How the reframe works:** Addiction is *defined* as continued use despite negative consequences.
Adding more consequences therefore applies the very thing the definition says already failed.
**Substitute:** remove barriers to help rather than add costs to use.
**Support:** "if we continue to use punishment to solve a problem that is defined by its resistance to punishment, we are not going to get very far."
**Directly contradicts:** tough-love and "let them hit bottom" advice, which remains widespread.

### T-19 — Don't wait for a bottom
**Source:** Maia Szalavitz. **Explicit negative finding on a widely-recommended thing.**
**Support:** "No, you don't have to hit bottom to get better."
**Note:** bottoms are sometimes fatal. Act anywhere on the curve.

### T-20 — Radical honesty as external memory
**Source:** Anna Lembke.
**What you literally do:** Say the exact quantity and frequency out loud to another person.
**Mechanism she gives:** speaking it makes it real in a way private knowledge is not — otherwise it
stays "pinging around in the dark recesses of my own mind."
**Echoes:** Coulter's sober-blog reading (T-4) and Burroughs's unsafe-neighbourhood rule (T-25) —
all three externalise cognition rather than trying to win an internal argument.

### T-21 — Treat the substrate, not just the behaviour
**Source:** Maia Szalavitz.
**What you literally do:** If the substance is regulating an underlying state (anxiety, depression),
treat that state directly. In her own case antidepressants came *before* the social recovery was
possible — and she notes she delayed five years because her 12-step program was hostile to
medication.
**Support:** "antidepressants were very important to my recovery."
**This is also a documented harm** of one recovery culture's norms.

### T-22 — Safety gate before any abstinence trial
**Source:** Anna Lembke. **Non-negotiable.**
Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can be life-threatening and require medical supervision, not a
self-directed fast. Any product feature that proposes a "30-day reset" must gate on substance first.
**Support:** "It's not safe for everybody…"

### T-23 — "Why the pain," not "why the addiction"
**Source:** Gabor Maté.
**How the reframe works:** Swaps a diagnostic question for an etiological one. It removes the moral
charge, so the person can answer honestly instead of defending. You stop interrogating the behaviour
and start asking what the behaviour is medicating.
**Support:** "my mantra is always not why the addiction, but why the pain?"
**Echoes:** Whitaker's and Warrington's question-swap (T-3) — three independent authors all replace
the accusatory question with a curious one. **This is the strongest cross-source convergence in the
entire document.**
**But see Axis G** — Fisher argues this frame gets over-extended into a single-cause story.

### T-24 — Work with the degree of choice you actually have
**Source:** Carl Erik Fisher.
**What you literally do:** Stop litigating whether you are powerless or responsible. Assess what
control is actually available in this specific situation and act at that level.
**Support:** "Recovery, in all its forms, becomes not about achieving perfect control—it's about skillfully working with whatever degree of choice we have."
**Position:** an explicit third way between the disease and choice camps.

### T-25 — "Your head is an unsafe neighbourhood"
**Source:** Augusten Burroughs.
**What you literally do:** Treat rumination as a *location* rather than a mood, and apply a location
rule: when you notice you have gone there, get another person on the phone or in the room.
Mechanically it converts an unwinnable internal argument into a trigger for a social action.
**Support:** "Think of your head as an unsafe neighborhood; don't go there alone." [RAW ×2 —
https://bookquoters.com/book/dry and https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/1825967-dry-a-memoir]
**One of the most product-ready lines in the corpus:** short, concrete, memorable, and it names both
a detection cue and an action.

### T-26 — Pray/intend for acceptance, not for outcomes
**Source:** Mary Karr.
**What you literally do:** Strip the wishlist out of prayer or intention-setting; ask only to accept
the reality you are in, then stay alert for *practical* next actions.
**Mechanism:** attention reallocation, from outcome-fantasy to available move.
**Support:** "Patti proposes that I pray to accept whatever reality I'm in, staying alert for practical solutions rather than issuing orders in prayer." [RAW]
**Note:** works in secular form; nothing in the mechanism requires the theology.

## 10. Additional disagreement axes (memoir and science lanes)

### Axis D — Does drinking make you interesting?
**Jamison demolishes the drunk-writer myth from inside her own memoir.** She says the true content of
her drinking was boredom: "a story about tedium, about claustrophobia and repetition." Set against
Knapp's "candle lit inside you" register and Hepola's "gasoline of all adventure," this is a genuine
split about whether the substance ever delivered what it promised. Jamison's answer is no; Knapp's
and Hepola's is that it did, and that this is exactly the problem.

### Axis E — Blackout, gender and agency
Hepola holds two positions many readers treat as incompatible: the gendered asymmetry is real ("when
men are in a blackout they do things to the world, but when women are in a blackout things are done
to them" — she is quoting an expert from her own book), *and* she refuses to surrender her own
agency ("I think you can be blacked out and consent"; "I roofed myself"). This is the most contested
single position in the corpus and should be handled carefully in any product context.

### Axis F — Is the spiritual component optional?
- **Karr:** achievable without, but she says she "would have been as mean as a snake."
- **Burroughs:** *Dry* reaches sobriety with no God-language at all, and mocks the machinery —
  "Meetings are the Hail Marys of alcoholics" — while also finding it the only safe disclosure venue.
- **Hepola:** initially recoiled, asking whether she would have to become "one of those frightening
  12-step people."
- **Jamison:** converts, but her objection was aesthetic, not religious — AA's insistence that
  everyone is the same was "basically a way of saying f\*ck you to my entire value system"
  (as quoted at https://www.bustle.com/p/the-recovering-by-leslie-jamison-contains-one-quote-about-love-that-i-cant-stop-thinking-about-8073276;
  Bustle censors the profanity, the book does not). **This is the sharpest articulation available of
  why intelligent people bounce off recovery programmes — and it is not a God objection.**

### Axis G — Is addiction caused by pain and disconnection?
- **For:** Maté ("why the pain"), Hari ("the opposite of addiction is… human connection").
- **Against the strong version:** Fisher, by name — single-cause attribution is "misleadingly
  reductionistic"; there is "no utopian future where fixing societal problems eradicates addiction."
- **Methodological:** Rat Park, the study underwriting the connection thesis, is contested — an
  Outline piece reports uncontrolled variables (isolated rats separated by sex while Rat Park rats
  were housed together and bred), data lost to equipment failure, and a 1996 failure to replicate.
  https://theoutline.com/post/2205/this-38-year-old-study-is-still-spreading-bad-ideas-about-addiction
  [FETCHED by the science lane; not independently re-verified.]
- **Verdict for product use:** the connection framing is useful and humane, but must not be presented
  as settled science, and Hari should not be the citation.

### Axis H — Grief work vs. capability rebuild
Knapp's love/breakup frame (T-10) implies **mourning** as the core work. Hepola's
infrastructure frame (T-12) implies **rebuilding capability**. Both are well-argued by people who
succeeded. They imply different product surfaces, and a good product probably needs both.

### Axis I — Should relapse reset the count?
- **Szalavitz:** no. "Any positive change" counts; harm reduced is progress. A reset-to-zero clock
  erases prior work and makes further effort feel pointless.
- **Burroughs:** frames relapse as agentive — "You chose to relapse. You didn't have to."
- **AA convention and most habit apps:** yes, reset.
**This is a live product-design decision, not a philosophical aside.** Note it collides directly with
streak mechanics. (Consistent with the existing house verdict against streak counters recorded for
the quit-vice module.)

## 13. Techniques and reframes — method-book lane

### T-27 — The back-to-front timing that fools the subconscious
**Source:** William Porter. **The most mechanically complete explanation in the corpus.**
**The mechanism, step by step:**
1. Drink → relaxation → then anxiety, as the brain's counter-response outlasts the alcohol.
2. The next drink relieves that anxiety **quickly**; the anxiety had accumulated **slowly**.
3. The subconscious links causes to effects by temporal proximity, so it learns "drink relieves
   anxiety" and never learns "drink *caused* the anxiety."
**Support:** "the subconscious mind misses out this key piece of information…"
**Crucially, Porter says conscious understanding is not sufficient:** knowing it intellectually
"won't stop us wanting a drink because the subconscious mind will still be triggering a desire."
**Product implication:** psychoeducation alone will not do the work. This is an argument against
explain-it-once designs.

### T-28 — Split the trigger from the craving; only the second is optional
**Source:** William Porter.
**What you literally do:** Treat the one-second knee-jerk thought (subconscious trigger) as
involuntary and unimportant. The *spiral* — consciously fantasising about the drink — is where the
suffering lives, and Porter locates it entirely in the conscious mind, making it a decision point.
**Support:** "craving takes place entirely in the conscious mind, and therefore we face a conscious decision whether to crave or not."
**Echoes:** Pooley's wine witch (T-37) and Gray's addictive voice (T-38) do the same split by
personification instead of by mechanism.

### T-29 — Failed attempts actively deepen the addiction
**Source:** William Porter. **A serious design warning.**
Porter's model holds that each failed quit attempt teaches the subconscious that without a drink you
literally cannot function. Failure is not neutral; it is instruction in the wrong direction.
**Product implication:** a tool that manufactures repeated visible failures is harmful on this model.
Directly relevant to streak mechanics — and it converges with Szalavitz's T-19/Axis I from a
completely different theoretical direction.

### T-30 — The sleep swap
**Source:** William Porter.
**What you literally do:** Replace "alcohol helps me sleep" with a concrete equivalent action:
"Drinking alcohol is the equivalent of setting an alarm for three o'clock in the morning."
Concrete, testable against the person's own experience.

### T-31 — Remove the desire instead of resisting it
**Source:** Allen Carr; adopted wholesale by Annie Grace.
**How the reframe works:** Willpower quitting leaves the desire intact, so the quitter spends life
wanting a forbidden thing — permanent deprivation, and permanent vulnerability. Carr's move is to
attack the *belief* that the substance delivers a benefit, on the theory that desire cannot survive
the belief's collapse.
**Support:** Carr — "the key to being a happy non-smoker is to remove the desire to smoke. With no
desire to smoke, it takes no Willpower not to do so." Grace — "Without desire, there is no
temptation. Without temptation, there is no addiction."
**Carr's failure mode for willpower methods** is stated directly: quitters "spend the rest of their
lives desperately moping for something that they desperately hope they will never have." [GR]

### T-32 — Kill the Big Monster first
**Source:** Allen Carr.
**What you literally do:** Split the addiction into two objects — the **Little Monster** (faint
physical withdrawal in the body) and the **Big Monster** (the belief system that interprets that
faintness as "I want one"). You do **not** fight the Little Monster; that is the willpower method.
You dismantle the beliefs, after which the physical pang is meaningless noise.
**Support:** "I have described the physical addiction as the Little Monster in the body. I describe
the mental addiction as the Big Monster in the mind." [FETCHED, official site]
Ordering stated in *Quit Drinking Without Willpower*: kill the Big Monster first, then the Little
Monster is easy. [GR]

### T-33 — The "tight shoes" audit
**Source:** Allen Carr.
**What you literally do:** For each supposed benefit, ask what state the substance returns you to.
The answer is always the baseline a non-user occupies for free.
**Support:** "like forcing yourself to wear tight shoes just to get the pleasure of taking them off."
**Echoes:** this is the same claim Porter derives mechanistically (T-27) and that Whitaker reached
independently via Carr (§4). **Carr states it as an image; Porter proves it as a mechanism** — they
are the memorable and the rigorous versions of one idea.

### T-34 — Build a portfolio of 30 tools, not one method
**Source:** Catherine Gray. **Explicitly anti-single-method.**
**What you literally do:** Try everything, keep what works, accumulate a personal toolkit mixing the
trivial and the serious. Gray's own list runs from long baths and crying to addictive voice
recognition and a 100-day challenge.
**Support:** "Everyone should just try everything, and keep what works, and they'll find their 30
tools that will help them." She also advises treating it "like you're studying for a degree."
**Product implication:** argues for a menu with a keep/discard mechanic, not a prescribed programme.

### T-35 — Daily external check-in
**Source:** Catherine Gray.
**What you literally do:** Email a named human every single day stating sober-or-slipped. Gray is
explicit that the *obligation to report to a person*, not the number, was the active ingredient.
**Support:** "That simple shift, of having to check in and be honest, was a seismic one for me."
**Echoes:** Lembke's radical honesty (T-20). Note the distinction from a streak counter: the
mechanism is a *person*, not a *number*.

### T-36 — Disclose asynchronously, through a one-way door
**Source:** Catherine Gray. **A negative finding plus its fix.**
In-person disclosure produced "teeth-grittingly bad conversations." Her fix: send a text or email
*before* seeing the person, specifically because hitting send is irreversible.
**Support:** "Done. No undoing it. No chickening out."
**Note the direct disagreement with McKowen's T-2** (tell nobody). Gray makes disclosure work by
changing its *channel*; McKowen solves the same problem by removing disclosure entirely.

### T-37 — Name and personify the urge
**Source:** Clare Pooley ("the wine witch").
**What you literally do:** Give the internal voice a name and a personality so you register it as
not-you.
**Why it works, in her words:** "You can't keep fighting yourself for very long, but you can fight an
evil enemy."
**She also documents the dynamics:** the voice gets *louder* immediately after quitting and quiets
around day 100 — but "just one drink brings them back to life with a vengeance." Forecasting this
shape is the same move as Lembke's T-16.

### T-38 — Addictive Voice Recognition
**Source:** Catherine Gray (who credits Belle's blog, not herself).
**What you literally do:** Separate out the voice saying "it would be a fabulous idea to have a
drink" / "what about now?" and treat it as a distinct speaker. [GR]
Same family as T-37 and T-28.

### T-39 — Close the moderation door first
**Source:** Clare Pooley — the first of her three stated preconditions, in order.
**What you literally do:** Settle, in advance, that moderation is off the table. **Mechanism she
names:** any residual hope of moderating hands the wine witch a script at the first hurdle.
Her diagnostic is a self-experiment: try one small glass a day and see whether it drives you crazy;
check how previous Dry Januarys ended; check whether you were ever a "social" user of anything.
Her other two preconditions are **connection** (she used blogging and Soberistas, not AA — "I'd think
about how I'd feel if I let them down") and **belief/excitement** (she claims she can predict success
from whether the person's email reads "Today is Day One and I am SO EXCITED!" or "I don't think I can").
**Corroborated by Gray**, quoting neuroscientist Alex Korb: "If you're failing to moderate, then
abstinence is the best option. It's easier to be abstinent than moderate." [GR]
**Pooley's own failed-moderation log** — no drinking till February (lasted a week), weekends only (a
week), out-only-never-home, beer-only — is the best documented "what didn't work" in the corpus.

### T-40 — Reframe *before* you start, not during
**Source:** Clare Pooley.
**What you literally do:** Read a method book to change the deprivation framing *before* day one,
because "If you start on this journey thinking that you are depriving yourself of something, then you
will never be happy sober."
**Note:** Pooley names Jason Vale, "Alan Carr's book" (sic — she misspells Allen Carr) and Grace's
*This Naked Mind*. **This is direct documentary evidence of the memoir → method-book pipeline, from a
memoirist** — and it is the second independent instance, after Holly Whitaker crediting Carr (§4).

## 14. Final disagreement axes

### Axis J — Is quitting permanent liberation or permanent maintenance?
**The deepest split in the entire corpus, and it is unbridgeable.**

| | Carr / Grace / Porter | Keyes / AA tradition |
|---|---|---|
| End state | Free. Desire removed. Nothing given up. | Alcoholic for life, one day at a time. |
| Ongoing work | None once the belief collapses | Meetings, indefinitely |
| Evidence | "there is nothing to 'give up'" | "It doesn't go away… But meetings work for me." |

Keyes at 30+ years sober still attends meetings; Carr's entire brand is *Without Willpower* and a
finished, one-time liberation. Both camps contain people with long, successful sobriety. **The
product cannot split the difference by wording — it has to either pick one, support both, or let the
user choose.**

Note the **lineage** on the left-hand column: Grace's "you sincerely enjoy ending the nuisance of
wanting a drink" is structurally identical to Carr's "what you really enjoy… is the ending of the
irritation of wanting that drink," and Porter supplies the mechanism both assert. Treat Carr → Grace
as one tradition with three voices, **not** as three independent confirmations.

### Axis K — Tell everyone, tell one person, or tell nobody?
- **Tell nobody:** McKowen (T-2) — declarations re-import *forever*.
- **Tell one person, daily, in writing:** Gray (T-35).
- **Tell them, but asynchronously:** Gray again (T-36) — in-person failed for her.
- **Tell a community, publicly:** Pooley (blogging as the accountability engine).
Four successful quitters, four incompatible disclosure protocols. **Strong evidence that disclosure
style should be a user preference, not a prescribed step.**

### Axis L — Does trauma explain it?
- **Maté:** yes, essentially — "why the pain."
- **Porter:** no, and on first-person empirical grounds — "I saw too many people out there with much
  more reason to drink than I did who didn't develop an alcohol problem."
- **Fisher:** neither; single-cause attribution is "misleadingly reductionistic."
Porter and Maté are the two clearest opposed poles, and Fisher is the published referee.

### Convergences worth more than any single source

Where authors who **disagree about everything else** land on the same move, that move is the safest
bet in the document:

1. **Swap the accusatory question for a curious one** — Maté ("why the pain"), Whitaker and
   Warrington ("is it doing anything for me?"), Grace (reject the binary label). Four authors, three
   traditions, mutually hostile on the disease model.
2. **Remove *wanting to* from the critical path** — Coulter (T-5), Karr and Jamison (T-11), Gray
   ("I didn't want to not drink"), McKowen (T-1). Action precedes motivation in every case.
3. **Externalise the internal argument** — Burroughs (T-25), Pooley (T-37), Gray (T-38),
   Porter (T-28), Lembke (T-20). Five authors, four traditions.
4. **Forecast the shape of the difficulty in advance** — Lembke (T-16), Pooley (day-100 curve),
   Jamison (T-15, relief expires), Coulter (T-7, build the muscle).
5. **Do not reset to zero on relapse** — Szalavitz (Axis I) and Porter (T-29), from opposite
   theoretical starting points, both say repeated visible failure makes things worse.
