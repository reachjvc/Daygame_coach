# Synthesis — what fifteen sources agree on, and what it means for the product

**Corpus:** 178,000 words, 2,186 quotes, 778 URLs, fifteen independently-gathered
sources. Every quote verified as an exact substring of raw fetched text;
attribution separately re-checked; four agents ran their own verifiers and one
ran a negative control with deliberately fabricated quotes to prove the verifier
caught them.

**What the verification found, which is itself a result:** one hallucinated
quote produced by the page-fetch layer, one composite quote stitched from two
separate passages, one quote containing a phrase absent from its source, two
genuine quotes attributed to the wrong people, and 53+ academic quotes that read
as verbatim testimony but are paraphrased by the papers' own stated policy. All
discarded. **Substring-verifying a quote does not verify who said it** — that
needs a second, separate pass, and two agents found real misattributions only
after running one.

## Part 1 — The ranking

Ranked by how many *independent* sources name a technique. Lineage is
discounted: Carr → Grace → Porter is one idea with three authors, and the
12-step family propagates through many of the peer sources, so agreement inside
either lineage counts once.

### Tier 1 — named by six or more independent sources

**1. Remove the supply and add friction to access.** (8 sources + the
strongest study-backed mechanism claim in the corpus.) Not willpower — physical
removal, including the residue routes people forget: grinder scrapings, the
saved card, the dealer's number, the app that can be re-offloaded. The
qualitative literature backs this harder than anything else: *environment beats
disposition*, five studies, with Robins' Vietnam cohort as the extreme case —
same men, same drug, changed setting, 20% withdrawal-symptomatic in-country
versus under 1% re-addicted at home.

**2. Change the context, not the intention.** (7 sources.) Drive the other way
home. Do not enter the aisle. Do not do the grocery shop at all for a month.
Change the room before you stop, not after — one account warns that rearranging
*after* just makes the new arrangement the new drinking spot. Untreated
resolvers name changing contexts as the top theme at 69.2%.

**3. The counter is a shame machine — refuse it.** (7 sources, from camps that
agree on nothing else.) Two memoirists from opposite theoretical positions;
craving-post volume that decays exponentially *but spikes on exactly the 30-day
and one-year marks*; a person who relapsed on the six-month mark; another at 495
days writing *"Clean time can become an addiction like anything else"*; entire
forum posts titled "Badge reset". Users propose their own replacement:
**measure recovery time after a lapse, not time since.**

**4. Shrink the unit of time.** (6 sources.) Not forever. Today, or the next
hour. *"Forget forever."* The daily pledge is the single core ritual of the
largest peer community, and it is deliberately non-milestone.

**5. Play the tape forward.** (6 sources.) *"What exactly are you planning to DO
while high?"* Notably, the standard clinical term — urge surfing — is nearly
absent from the largest peer corpus (4 mentions) while this is explicitly taught
to newcomers. It also maps onto the one mechanism here with a real trial behind
it, episodic future thinking.

### Tier 2 — four to five independent sources

**6. Tell exactly one person.** Not a public declaration. One account of
"burning the ships" — telling boss, HR and parents in a single morning —
**relapsed at day 30**. Disclosure protocol is genuinely contested (four
successful quitters used four incompatible ones), but *one person* recurs far
more than *everyone*.

**7. A rehearsed one-line refusal, with no reason attached.** A reason is
something to argue with. 315 mentions in one corpus alone.

**8. Delegate the constraint to somebody else.** The cross-behaviour finding:
gambling blockers are defeated by offshore sites, VPNs and app-offloading;
porn blockers *"work only when someone else holds the key"*. Money separation to
a trusted person outranks every piece of software. **A constraint you can revoke
alone is not a constraint.**

**9. Identity relabel — "I don't", not "I'm not".** The one part of the
habit-framework canon that transfers to addiction; the habit-loop machinery
itself gets explicitly rejected for it. The qualitative caveat matters:
identity change is about *becoming someone*, not about labelling the problem.

**10. A specific, named, pre-stocked replacement.** Not "find alternatives" — a
named drink you actually like, an ordered stack of things for the 5pm–11pm
window. Contested for alcohol-free substitutes specifically.

**11. Exercise at the moment of craving.** 282 mentions in one corpus. The
low-barrier version people actually do is a long walk with a podcast.

**12. Service — help somebody worse off than you, while still unstable.** Four
studies, and the single most cross-organisational practice in mutual aid. Giving
support beats receiving it.

### Tier 3 — high value, low current adoption

**13. A pre-committed tripwire, written while calm.** Only **two people in the
entire corpus** had one. Nobody who failed did. This is the largest unmet
opportunity in the whole set.

**14. Read other people's accounts at the moment of craving.** Rated the
most-valued feature (80.8%) in the one community with genuinely good outcomes;
its live chat was rated *least* valuable (19.6%). Needs no setup, works on day
one.

## Part 2 — The finding that outranks the ranking

**Eight sources, five substances, forums and peer review: the hazard is the good
stretch, not the bad night.**

- Cannabis: *"now I can finally moderate"*, arriving predictably at ~10 days and ~2 months
- Blogs and YouTube: day 4, when symptoms lift and a reward-bargain appears
- Nicotine: 6 months, 1 year, and one case at 9 years
- Podcasts: months 3–9, the genre's admitted blind spot
- Mutual aid: members phone each other on ordinary days, not bad ones
- Harm reduction: five accounts, one explicitly ruling out the obvious confound —
  *"It wasn't even stress or trauma: I just thought I deserved a treat"*
- And a mechanism from an RCT: **positive affect predicted urge, which predicted
  intoxication** (Bold 2016)

**The signal that distinguishes durable from fragile: whether the plan rests on
self-trust or on structure.** One person failed at day 72 *because* confidence
returned. Another has held for months while saying *"I still crave it every day
and can't really trust myself"* — with a timed lock box enforcing the gap.

**Treat "I'll be fine" as a red flag, not a green one.**

## Part 3 — Answering the ten-hour question

The brief was: given ten hours, how do we best raise someone's awareness of how
to change? The evidence answers a slightly different question than the one
asked, and the honest version is:

**Not ten hours in the app.**

- Engagement volume predicts nothing good across three independent designs; in
  one, posting more was *positively* associated with a use episode
- A high *proportion* of recovery-community membership carries **OR 5.00** for a
  use episode; breadth of ordinary non-recovery life carries **OR 0.25**. The
  harmful variable is narrowing, not the content
- The community with good outcomes reports **1–3 hours per week**
- The work that matters happens *before* day one: across people who had failed
  many times, what was different was that **the internal argument was over
  before they started — less need for willpower, not more.** Conspicuously
  absent: more motivation, more health information

So the ten hours get spent like this:

| Hours | Where | What | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | In app | The accurate picture: the count, the arithmetic, the trajectory | 95% who meet criteria and get no help say they did not think they needed any |
| 3–5 | **Out of app**, app as checklist | Physically change the environment: dispose, block, hand the key to a person, change the route, move the furniture *before* stopping | Tier-1 techniques 1 and 2; the strongest mechanism claim in the corpus |
| 6 | Out of app | Tell exactly one person; write the line you will say | Tier 2; and the public-declaration version has a documented failure |
| 7 | In app | Write the tripwire — aimed at the **good stretch**, not the bad night | The largest unmet gap; nobody who failed had one |
| 8 | In app | One or two vivid, dated future scenes. Not eight | Play the tape forward; the EFT evidence needs one good cue, not a set |
| 9–10 | **Distributed, never banked** | ~10 minutes a week for a year: the refresher, the good-stretch check, other people's accounts | Reframes decay; 1–3 hrs/week is the dose that works |

**Two design consequences that follow directly:**

1. **The app should cap and eject.** The study's own authors recommend
   platforms point members *out* toward non-recovery interests. A product
   optimising time-in-app is optimising a variable the evidence associates with
   worse outcomes.
2. **Build the refresher, not more content.** The reframe demonstrably decays —
   Carr worked for three years, then eight months, then not at all; expectancy
   challenge fades within about a month. Users improvise their own re-reads and
   the vendor charges for it. Software is the only medium that can do it
   properly, and nobody has.

## Part 4 — What this says about what is already built

| Already right, and now better evidenced | Source count |
|---|---|
| No streak counter | 7 |
| No promise of urge duration | reinforced — 3 sources say do not promise *any* timing |
| Withdrawal safety gate before a date | validated; 27.8% of Dry January registrants screened as possibly dependent |
| Refusal script, short, no reason | 4 |
| If-then plans, negation rejected | mechanism confirmed |
| Naming and answering the urge | convergently invented by four separate camps |
| Awareness flow existing at all | the pre-decision work is the work |

| Needs changing | Why |
|---|---|
| **Urge tool is built on urge surfing** | Large failure literature: it can *extend* the urge, the urge can be too fast to catch, and it is a skill that assumes stability beginners lack. A practitioner recommends redirecting *away* in cue-rich settings. Play-the-tape-forward is what the peer corpus actually teaches — and the module already has it, buried in a 25-minute flow |
| **Incongruence question buckets at intake** | The distress types are a trajectory, not a taxonomy — people move cost→model→the-model-is-the-distress. Bucketing gets exactly those people wrong |
| **Nothing addresses the good stretch** | Eight sources. Every tool fires at the bad moment |
| **Refusal line hardcoded to drinking** | Wrong for five of nine vices, shown unconditionally on the card |
| **Self-administered binding options** | A constraint you can revoke alone is not a constraint |
| **Futures screen asks for eight cues** | The evidence needs one or two |
| **No testimonial surface at all** | Highest-rated feature in the one community with good outcomes; needs no setup, so it fixes the cold-start hole |

## Part 5 — Standing cautions

- **Survivorship bias is the corpus's central limit.** Almost everything here is
  written by people for whom it worked; people who quietly drift back stop
  posting. One partial exception: r/stopdrinking normalises posting relapses.
- **Three numbers will be quoted at us and are false as stated:** Allen Carr's
  "90%" is a refund-non-claim rate (trials: 19.4% and 22%); the Sinclair
  Method's "78%" cannot be sourced anywhere, including Sinclair's own abstract;
  Brewer's "5x" is one small in-person trial advertising an app whose own RCT
  was null.
- **Licensing:** Recovery Dharma is CC BY-NC and cannot ship in a paid product.
  Allen Carr's Easyway holds live marks in classes 9/41/44 — software, training,
  health — and files DMCA notices in volume. Rational Recovery and AVRT marks
  are cancelled but that is not abandonment; the avoid rule stands.
- **Gaps we did not paper over:** Granfield & Cloud and Biernacki unobtainable
  by two independent routes, so zero quotes from either despite both being
  canonical. GamCare Cloudflare-blocked, which is likely why Monzo is the only
  UK bank named. CDC Tips unreachable; zero quotes drawn.
