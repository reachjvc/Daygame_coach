### 0.3 Advocacy bias — read this before using any quote

These are **not** naturalistic accounts of behaviour change. Every one of them is a **published artefact of an organisation that wants more members and more money**, and the selection pressures are visible in the text itself:

1. **Survivorship, by construction.** Each org publishes people for whom its method worked. Nobody publishes the person who attended eleven meetings and vanished. The base rate of the method is invisible in the corpus.
2. **Several are literally fundraising copy.** SMART's Caitlin story ends: *"Your year-end gift to our Growth Fund will be put to work immediately"* and *"DOUBLE Your Impact: Your tax-deductible gift, made before midnight on December 31, will be matched 100%"* (smartrecovery.org/blog/a-rock-n-roll-recovery-story-part-2-of-2). The Loretta story ends the same way. The narrative arc is shaped to land on a donate button.
3. **Comparative framing is structural.** The LifeRing corpus is dominated by "AA didn't work, LifeRing did" because that is the recruiting story LifeRing exists to tell. The *content* of those complaints is still real data — but the *ratio* is manufactured.
4. **In-house vocabulary leaks into the testimony.** SMART members write "VACI", "HOV", "CBA"; WFS members write "New Life"; RD members write "sangha". People narrate their change in the dialect they were given, which makes it hard to tell the mechanism from the branding.
5. **Anonymity traditions destroy verifiability by design.** "Nancy A." cannot be followed up. Sober-time claims are self-reported and unaudited.
6. **Nobody publishes the person the programme injured.** The harm accounts in §4 are almost all *lateral* — collected by a *rival* org, or by a dissident faction inside AA. There is no "here is who we hurt" page anywhere in this corpus.

**Rule for downstream use: tag every quote with the org, and treat the causal claim ("X worked because of Y") as the org's hypothesis, not a finding. The behavioural detail — what the person physically did on a Tuesday — is the reliable part.**

### 0.4 Where the philosophies directly contradict each other

| Axis | Pole A | Pole B |
|---|---|---|
| **Agency** | AA 1939: *"kept that way by submitting my natural will to the Higher Power"* (The Salesman) | Recovery Dharma: *"this is a program of empowerment, not powerlessness"* (Amy). WFS: *"I now take charge of my life and my well-being"*. LifeRing: *"The credit for each day of your sobriety belongs to you."* SMART: "self-empowered" is in the org's own boilerplate. |
| **Goal** | Abstinence-only — AA, SMART (mostly), LifeRing (*"zero consumption"*), WFS (*"abstinence-based"*), SOS, Recovery Dharma | Moderation Management: *"a scientifically-based harm reduction program which recognizes that reorienting our relationship to alcohol can be accomplished with…"* (Stephanie J.). Member Brian moderates to a rule set and calls it success. |
| **Spirituality** | AA 1939 is saturated: God appears in nearly every story; the compiler's preface even complains that modern stories *"do not even MENTION a Higher Power"* | SOS, LifeRing, SMART, WFS are explicitly secular. Recovery Dharma is Buddhist but non-theistic: *"I don't so much believe in a “higher power,” but rather a deeper power"* (Rick Millman). |
| **Identity** | AA: lifelong "I am an alcoholic", disease framing, daily renewal | WFS statement 1 is past-tense: *"I have a life-threatening problem that once had me."* LifeRing's toolbox opens by denying a programme exists at all. |
| **Meeting mechanics** | AA: no crosstalk, sequential shares, no advice | LifeRing: *"its meetings encourage crosstalk"* (David) — several members name crosstalk as **the** reason they stayed. SMART meetings are facilitator-led and tool-driven. |
| **Direction-taking** | Mary M. (AA, ~40 yrs): *"I was to attend a meeting every single day. No exceptions, no excuses. I didn't fight, I didn't argue. I just blindly followed. Did what I was told."* | LifeRing: *"Ordinarily, no one will offer to tell you what to do, or give you advice, unless you expressly ask for it."* |
| **Relapse** | AA's day-count culture (Grapevine's site ships a *"Sobriety Calculator"*) | LifeRing: *"a relapse may be a key part of your learning experience. “A fall into the pit, a gain in your wit.”"* SMART's Caitlin: *"Even after relapsing several times, she never felt like she was a failure or had to start over."* |
| **Talking about the past** | The AA drunkalogue — the 1939 stories are 80% drinking history | Recovery Dharma's stories dwell far less on using history and more on present practice; SMART's published stories are structured around tools rather than drinking narrative. (Characterisation of the fetched texts, not a quote.) |

The single sharpest collision is **agency**, and it is not an abstract dispute — Recovery Dharma's Amy names trauma as the reason the word is unusable for her:

> "As a writer, I know that words matter. As a survivor of trauma among so many other survivors of trauma, I know that powerlessness is not what I want my recovery to be based on."
> — Amy, *Recovery Dharma*, 2nd ed. (2023), p. 60. CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. PDF via recoverydharma.org/book/

## 1. Testimonials — Alcoholics Anonymous

### 1.1 The 1939 first edition (public domain in the US)

**Provenance and a privacy note.** Source: `https://aainthedesert.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/FIRST-EDITION-BIG-BOOK-STORIES.pdf` — a compilation hosted by an AA intergroup, prefaced by an unofficial editor. Cross-checked against `https://silkworth.net/alcoholics-anonymous/15-the-salesman/`; the two match word-for-word on the passage tested (§5.1). **In the 1939 book these stories are unsigned — title only.** The PDF adds authors' full names, supplied by later AA historians. Per the privacy rule I cite by **story title only** and do not propagate those names.

**T1 — "The Salesman" (1939 1st ed., first-edition-only story).** The whole self-treatment mechanism in one paragraph — being *put to work on someone else* while still shaky:

> "There is another part I want to talk about-a very important part. I think I would have had much more difficulty in getting straightened out if I hadn't been almost immediately put to work. I don't mean getting back on my job as a salesman. I mean something that is necessary to my continued happiness. While I was still shakily trying to rebuild my job of selling, the doctor sent me to see another alcoholic who was in the hospital. All the doctor asked me to do was tell my story."

And the daily practice, plus the explicit anti-graduation clause:

> "Every morning I read a part of the Bible and ask God to carry me through the day safely."

> "That submission wasn't just a single act, however. It became a daily duty; it had to be that. Daily I am renewed in strength and I have never come to the point where I have wanted to say, “Thanks, God, I think I can paddle my own canoe now,” for which I am thankful."

His first meeting **did not work** — worth noting, because the org's own literature preserved the failure:

> "I say that the meeting did not impress me. However, I could see men who I had known as good, hard-working drunkards apparently in their right minds, but I just couldn't see where I came into the picture. I went home, stayed sober for a few days, but was soon back to my regular quota of liquor every day."

He came back **six months later**.

**T2 — "A Feminine Victory" (1939 1st ed., first-edition-only; a woman's story).** The single most transferable behaviour in the entire AA corpus — *phoning someone mid-relapse, two drinks in*:

> "This time I went to the telephone (after I had taken about two drinks) and called L to tell her what I had done. She asked me to promise that I would not take another drink before someone came to me. Well, I had learned enough about truthfulness to refuse to give that promise. Had I been living after the old pattern, I would have been ashamed to call for help. In fact I should not have wanted help. I should have tried to hide the fact that I was drinking and continued until I again wound up behind the "eight ball.""

Note what she says the *change* was: not willpower, but the disappearance of the shame that used to make concealment automatic. Note also that she **refused to promise** and was helped anyway.

**T3 — "The European Drinker" (1939 1st ed.).** Names the three ingredients in order, and puts the doctrine last:

> "It wasn't very long until my doctor friend sent me to tell another alcoholic what my experience had been. This duty together with my weekly meetings with my fellow alcoholics and my daily renewal of the contract I originally made with God have kept me sober when nothing else ever did."

**T4 — "The Fearful One" (1939 1st ed.).** Service framed as the payload, not the byproduct:

> "I know that incomparable happiness that comes from helping some other fellow get straightened out. Don't get me wrong. We are not a bunch of angels. None of us has any notion of becoming such."

**T5 — "Our Southern Friend" (1939 1st ed.).** Written in present tense; reads like a log. The two operative moves are *go and be useful to someone* and *say the plan out loud to a person who won't panic*:

> "Get into the lives of other people, is one thing the fellows in New York had said. I go to see a man I had been asked to visit and tell him my story. I feel much better! I have forgotten about a drink."

> "I'll get drunk! It is a cold-blooded idea. It is premeditated. I fix up a little apartment over the garage with books and drinking water. … Halfway down the driveway a thought strikes me. I'll be honest anyway. I'll tell my wife what I am going to do. I back up to the door and go into the house. I call my wife into a room where we can talk privately. I tell her quietly what I intend to do. She says nothing. She does not get excited. She maintains a perfect calm. When I am through speaking, the whole idea has become absurd. Not a trace of fear is in me. I laugh at the insanity of it."

This is the clearest first-person description in the corpus of **speaking a relapse plan aloud to a non-reactive listener, and the plan collapsing under its own absurdity**. The listener's behaviour is specified: she says nothing, does not get excited, stays calm.

**T6 — "The Doctor's Nightmare" (1939 1st ed.).** For contrast: two and a half years of studying the spiritual solution while still drinking nightly.

> "I gave the matter much time and study for the next two and a half years, but still got tight every night nevertheless. I read everything I could find, and talked to everyone who I thought knew anything about it."

Insight was not sufficient. Useful counterweight to any product that assumes understanding drives change.

### 1.2 AA Grapevine, September 2026 "Sponsorship" issue

Free opening paragraphs only (full text subscriber-gated). Attribution convention preserved exactly.

**T7 — M.G., Los Angeles, California — "Now Hiring"** (aagrapevine.org/magazine/2026/sep/now-hiring). The clearest published account of a **sponsor relationship going wrong**:

> "I didn't know you were allowed to fire a sponsor. I thought once you picked one, you were locked in; that resistance meant ego, and ego meant danger. So when I started to feel confused and controlled in the sponsor relationship, I assumed the problem was me."

**T8 — Mike W., Bellingham, Washington — "My Temporary Sponsor"** (…/my-temporary-sponsor). Editor's standfirst plus the opening line; establishes that **time-boxed sponsorship is a recognised form**:

> "Fresh out of treatment, he desperately asked for a sponsor. One guy stepped up, but only for a while. Oh really?"
> "Many members believe they have the best sponsor ever, but I really do. It's true."

**T9 — Betsy S., Tucson, Arizona — "My Wild Card Sponsor"** (…/my-wild-card-sponsor). A 28-year relationship that started as triage:

> "What started as an emergency fix for a five-alarm situation became an incredible 28-year relationship"
> "When I was eight years sober I married someone 10 years younger than me. He was three years sober and fresh out of Navy tech school, and we moved to our duty station in rural Florida."

**T10 — Justin B., Pompano Beach, Florida — "Get in the Car"** (…/get-car). The sponsor as an agent who **physically shows up and removes the choice**:

> "He was four months sober, fresh out of jail and trying to avoid his sponsor. Good try!"
> "It was a Saturday morning in November of 2019, and I had just been released from jail. I had four months sober. I was back at my home group for the first time, and I felt completely detached from my peers in the room."

**T11 — Chris B., Oakdale, Louisiana — "Learning to Trust"** (…/learning-trust). Recovery inside long-term incarceration:

> "Doing time and trying to work a program can be really hard some days. Here at our Oakdale correctional facility, most members of our AA home group are serving long-term sentences The most paramount thing in our lives is trying to cope with incarceration."

**T12 — Allison R., New York, New York — "Never Too Young"** (…/never-too-young):

> "By the time I discovered the miracle of alcohol, I already felt like I had catching up to do. I was 14 years old, and I needed that drink. Before alcohol, I found other coping mechanisms to deal with these feelings—achievement, perfectionism, people pleasing and lying."

### 1.3 AA Agnostica (dissident-but-inside AA)

**T13 — Mary M., "They Built Me", 15 March 2026** (aaagnostica.org/2026/03/15/they-built-me/). ~40 years sober. **The richest mechanical account in the whole corpus** — and note that not one of the mechanisms she names is a Step:

> "So in the afternoon I made That Call and a woman showed up at my door within an hour and said she was going to drive me to my first meeting. I thought she was an employee of head office. And she laughed and said, No, I'm a drunk like you! I was gobsmacked. She had 2 years without a drink. Two. Whole Years."

> "And there they were. A group of drunks like me, mothering and fathering me. Pushing me upright. Taking me out after meetings for coffee and stringing the pieces of my life together. Making lists. Top of the list was get a job. Then talk to the mortgage company. Talk to the kids. Get a haircut. Clean myself up. A tiny step at a time. I was a child. I was to share the emotional turmoil of each day with a trusted one of them so they could help me sort myself out. I was beyond labelling feelings. I was numb. I was to attend a meeting every single day. No exceptions, no excuses. I didn't fight, I didn't argue. I just blindly followed. Did what I was told."

> "All with a newfound belief in myself given to me so freely, so long ago, by a Group Of Drunks. My G.O.D."

Her bio line, published with the piece, also documents cross-belief sponsorship in both directions:

> "She has sponsored many over the years, Jews, Muslims, Hindu, devout Christians, etc. And had a devout Christian sponsor for over thirty years – now deceased."

**T14 — Pat N., comment on "Another Agnostic Group Ousted", 6 May 2012** (aaagnostica.org/another-agnostic-group-ousted/). 32 years sober; testifies that for a subpopulation the secular variant is **the difference between attending and not attending at all**:

> "I've been sober 32 years, primarily through the We Agnostic group in Olympia, Washington, USA. … I've heard MANY newcomers say they wouldn't come to AA at all if we didn't exist, and some regular attendees have 20 years m/l."

## 2. Testimonials — the alternatives

### 2.1 SMART Recovery

**T15 — Caitlin Darfler**, "A Rock 'N' Roll Recovery Story", Parts 1 & 2, 19 & 28 Dec 2018 (smartrecovery.org/blog/a-rock-n-roll-recovery-story-part-1-of-2). Eight rehab admissions at four institutions before she found SMART. Her stated reason it landed is a **reframe of the moral model**:

> "SMART is a program that teaches coping skills with actual tools based in proven behavioral therapies," said Caitlin. "It looks at maladaptive behaviors as the human condition and not character defects, and that was instrumental for my recovery and self-awareness."

> "It took me two years and several trips to rehab to find SMART Recovery," said Caitlin "I was drowning in my addiction and until I found SMART Recovery, there was no way out."

Multi-pathway, as reported by SMART: *"For her first six months off of heroin, she was prescribed Vivitrol… She utilized therapy… And she incorporated SMART meetings into her recovery plan."* And on relapse: *"Even after relapsing several times, she never felt like she was a failure or had to start over."*

**T16 — ChrisTLynne**, "Pathways to Recovery: A Success Story", 12 Nov 2021 (smartrecovery.org/blog/pathways-to-recovery-a-success-story). Journal-format, ~8 months. Shows programme vocabulary becoming *household* vocabulary:

> "They know how important my recovery is (at this point acronyms like Hierarchy of Values (HOV) and Cost Benefit Analysis (CBA) are dropped on the daily in my house)"

> "After a nice, long shower I felt empowered and motivated to keep going the rest of the day – action really does precede motivation. I baked pumpkin bread before dinner (baking is one of my favorite Vital Absorbing Creative Interest (VACIs)), did a couple loads of laundry, and cleaned the house."

**T17 — Loretta Billingsley**, "On a Recovery Journey of Learning, Growing, & Healing", 2 Dec 2022 (smartrecovery.org/blog/on-a-recovery-journey-of-learning-growing-healing). Came in **not yet committed to changing** — an unusually honest entry point for a testimonial:

> "When Loretta Billingsley found SMART Recovery, she says she wasn't fully committed to addressing her troublesome alcohol use, but wanted to understand more about it."

> She says she sees women starting to listen to their "inner ally," rather than their "inner critic."

**T18 — Burk Jackson**, "Connection Doesn't Expire: What 31 Years in Recovery Taught Me", 23 Jun 2026 (smartrecovery.org/blog/connection-doesnt-expire-what-31-years-in-recovery-taught-me). Guest post by a member who is also promoting his own platform — bias flag — but it names a real failure mode of tool-based programmes:

> "You can have every cognitive strategy in your toolkit and still hit a wall at 9pm on a Tuesday that no worksheet reaches."

> "In early recovery, the community forms around you. The meetings, the sponsors, the check-ins — the support is visible and accessible. But somewhere around year three, or year seven, or year fifteen, the scaffolding quietly comes down. People assume you're fine. You assume you're supposed to be fine."

> "But self-empowerment doesn't mean self-isolation."

### 2.2 LifeRing Secular Recovery

All from `https://lifering.org/lifering-success-stories/` unless noted.

**T19 — Nancy A.** Long-form; the notable content is what she says the bond was, and that **relapse ran for years inside the programme**:

> "I struggled with relapses for a couple of years. My last drink was sometime in October of 2004 and my only regret is that I waited so long to quit."

> "I'll never forget the group of people who were there during the time period that I was. There's a bond there that nothing can break."

**T20 — Gil, Benicia CA.** Twenty-five years of drinking gated behind a doctrinal blocker:

After 25 years of hard drinking and many attempts at quitting I met LifeRing (then named SOS) about 13 years ago, and after one false start got sober Jan 7, 1997, and have remained so. 11 years yesterday. > "I also go to NA for the structure aspect. Doing both programs have helped me in so many ways. If I had to choose one over the other, LifeRing would win hands down. … We have a family at LifeRing and it helps to see the same faces every week, which tells me it works."

**T22 — Rick H., Hayward CA.** Explicitly refuses to trash the other fellowship, and isolates crosstalk:

> "Yes I needed structure in my life to help me with my new journey to stay clean, and the 12 step programs are life savers to those who choose them. LifeRing allows me the freedom of talking & sharing exactly when I need it. … Respectful crosstalk is what I love about LifeRing & yearn for in my recovery."

**T23 — David, San Rafael CA.** The clearest statement of the meeting's *active ingredient* as social rehearsal, not content:

> "I like LifeRing because its meetings encourage crosstalk, and thereby give recovering people the opportunity to experience normal group interaction soberly, and to practice social skills in a supportive environment."

**T24 — Bettye D., Madison TN.** Christian, but doctrinally injured by 12-step framing:

> "I am a Christian and proud of it but some of the principles they had put more doubt in my mind. With LifeRing I was able to be myself and still recover. I am proud to say I have 9 years clean… I went from a person looking for help, to a convenor and a woman with a message of hope"

**T25 — Mary Bradford, San Rafael CA.** Quit at 77; the account is unusually specific about *which* habits had to be engineered:

> "It came to me later…months later, in fact…that I truly believed I COULDN'T quit drinking. Every time I told myself to stop, I'd think: I can't! I love drinking. What'll I do at parties? The truth was there weren't that many parties; I was mostly drinking alone at home."

> "I learned how to prepare myself for parties knowing I wouldn't drink. I figured out how to break the five o'clock habit every day. I prepared for the sudden urges to drink. I learned to be honest in the meetings and to myself."

**T26 — Connie, Moraga CA.** A harm account (see §4):

> "Like so many others, the 12 step approach did not fit me at all! I became very depressed and my self-esteem diminished after each meeting. I knew there had to be something else."

**T27 — Gary P., Redondo Beach CA.** Eleven words on identity damage:

> "I think therefore I am … sick. That used to be my AA schema of the world. I found LifeRing and other secular sobriety programs and now I just think."

**T28 — Gail, Burlingame CA.** Isolates *support*, not method, as the relapse variable:

> "I could always go to detox and stay sober about a year, but then because I hated AA so much I couldn't face going to those meetings. And without support I'd always relapse."

**T29 — Dan K., Joliet IL.** The whole self-directed pitch in three sentences:

> "I don't like being told what to do. LifeRing met me on my terms and helped me stay focused. That's all I was looking for. It was a good fit."

**T30 — Donna, Berkeley CA.** Eight years; environment-as-default:

> "What LifeRing means to me is having a community that is built around and assumes sobriety. I've made lifelong personal friends and my weekly participation — now over 8 years — keeps me away from the 'slippery slope.'"

**T31 — Ralph, San Francisco.** Crossover from long AA service:

> "I have carried the sobriety message in fluent AA for a number of years — and have found that there is a real need for a different point of view and a different language. I have found both in LifeRing."

**T32 — James Gibson (chat name warpedone).** Belonging as the operative variable:

> "I tried 12 step programs but didn't fit in there. Then I found LifeRing. It's truly amazing. I fit here."

### 2.3 Women for Sobriety

**T33 — Rebecca W.** (womenforsobriety.org/new-life-program/). The only member testimonial published on the programme page:

> "Women for Sobriety works for me because it provides the tools and support I need to heal from my addiction and not only survive, but thrive in my recovery. The 13 statements of the New Life Program helped me shift from feeling I was at the mercy of my negative thoughts to using positive thinking to create a life that I love."

**T34 — Jean Kirkpatrick, Ph.D., founder** (womenforsobriety.org/about/founder/ and /new-life-program/). Founder testimony, published by the org, and the source of its break with AA:

> "We create our own world. No one else does. We are responsible for ourselves and our choices."

> "We begin by being in charge of our actions through our thoughts. We no longer react but now begin to act .. We end game playing. We become our whole person by learning independent thinking."

> "As women with addictions, we must learn about ourselves and how to avoid the pitfalls of faulty thinking and destructive behavior that used to lead us to taking that first drink or drug"

WFS's own framing of the founding grievance, on the same page: the programme *"nurture[s] feelings of self-value and self-worth, and the desire to discard feelings of guilt, shame and humiliation"* — i.e. it is built as a **corrective to a shame-based model**.

### 2.4 Recovery Dharma

Site story CC BY-NC 4.0; book CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.

**T35 — Rick Millman**, "Rick's Story of Recovery", 16 Jan 2020 (recoverydharma.org/rick-m/). Sober via AA in 1995, moved to Buddhist recovery ~2013. Explicitly **non-tribal about which programme works**:

> "There were no Buddhist recovery programs around at the time, so after a few attempts at going it alone, I realized I needed help from others. So, I got sober in Alcoholics Anonymous and got into substance abuse treatment. It worked–I truly believe that any program can work if I put the energy into it, so that's exactly what I did. That was back in 1995."

> "I don't so much believe in a “higher power,” but rather a deeper power than I can access from the inside as well as the collective wisdom of all you folks."

> "Then I heard of a fellowship that did not feel a need to use the 12 steps as the “middle man.”"

**T36 — Amy**, *Recovery Dharma* 2nd ed. (2023), pp. 58–61. Five years sober in the Bay Area, then **the programme failed to transfer geographically** — the strongest evidence in the corpus that the local subculture, not the method, was carrying the weight:

> "I had expected to just fall into 12-steps here and find a new friend group like I did back in California. But the meetings were weird, full of a southern Christian God and what seemed like questionable recovery. The old-timers sounded like cult leaders. The repetition of readings and sayings and slogans began to wear thin."

> "Then I found a flier on a coffee shop bulletin board for a Buddhist recovery group, and I decided to give it a try. The meeting was just a handful of quirky-looking people sitting on cushions in a circle on the floor, talking about their lives in what felt like a refreshingly intimate and authentic way. I immediately felt like this was the room I wanted to be in."

She is also generous about what 12-step gave her, which raises her credibility on the criticism:

> "I had a good early recovery in 12-steps, and I am grateful."

> "I learned how to be close to people. I learned how to take responsibility for my actions, and I learned that my actions affected other people. Somehow, that had never occurred to me."

**T37 — Chance**, *Recovery Dharma* 2nd ed., pp. 72–77. Got clean in county jail, served seven years of a fourteen-year sentence. Contains a rare **backfire account of meditation itself** (see §4) and the most concrete environmental hack in the corpus:

> "On one hand, I started going to 12-step meetings in the county jail, found a sponsor on the streets, and began the process of writing inventories and making amends. On the other hand, I started meditating as best as I knew how."

> "Every afternoon during count, loud buzzers would sound off in the cell block. So, every afternoon, half an hour before count time, I rolled up a blanket and sat on the floor of my cell. The prison would be my timer."

> "Since coming home, I have found that six things have been integral to my continued growth in recovery and as a human being. Meditation. Meetings. Study. Service. Friends. Teachers."

**T38 — Synyi**, *Recovery Dharma* 2nd ed., ~pp. 67–71. A literal daily-practice spec:

> "Here are some snapshots of my practice. Everyday, I sit down to meditate. Maybe it's 45 minutes, or 5 minutes, or somewhere in between. I heard a teacher once say that even just paying attention to 1 breath counts. After I meditate, I recite the Five Remembrances… Next I recite one round of classic metta phrases. I close my sit with taking refuge in the Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha."

> "I build on my daily sitting and life practice with residential practice periods. I do at least one 7-10 day silent meditation retreat per year"

### 2.5 Moderation Management

All from `https://moderation.org/testimonials/`.

**T39 — Brian, MM Facebook Group Member.** The most operationally detailed single account collected. Note it includes **medication as a pre-committed fallback**, a tolerance-reset, and a one-word in-head rule:

> "When I got back to the states, I decided to take MM seriously. I did 30 days AF, ordered the book Responsible Drinking, and started weekly therapy sessions with the plan that if I couldn't drink more moderately, I would take naltrexone. With the use of non-alcoholic beer, taking 3-4 days AF per week, I was able to bring down my tolerance to the point that 4 beers were more potent than 8 beers were previously."

> "Fast forward to last week, same trip, 11th anniversary. The phrase 'no shots' is in my head as I'm moderating my way through an all-inclusive resort."

**T40 — Stephanie J.** The org's positioning in a member's mouth:

> "In a 'recovery' world that sees only in black and white (total drunkenness or total abstinence), Moderation Management is a scientifically-based harm reduction program which recognizes that reorienting our relationship to alcohol can be accomplished with tried-and-true tips and non-judmental group support."

**T41 — Starfish, MM Listserv Member.** Explicitly holds both goals open — a direct contradiction of abstinence-only doctrine:

> "MM has given me something I've been missing for a long time: Hope. Hope that I can do this. I can't imagine a life entirely without alcohol, and now I know I don't have to. Armed with the tools necessary to feel empowered, I can abstain. And I can moderate. And I can make that decision for myself. Yes, I slip sometimes. But these slips aren't failures, they are moments to learn and move forward."

**T42 — Steph F., MM Facebook Member.** Names the mechanism as *scheduled, negotiable abstinent days plus honest reporting*:

> "I liked moderation month. It helped me get thru Feb with minimal alcohol. … I like the accountability and the ability to be honest when we juggle the ABS days around. I was/am a nightly drinker but really wanted to cut back. The tools have helped a lot."

**T43 — Cora, MM Listserv Member.** Maintenance framing, no graduation:

**T44 — Sarah H., MM Forum Member** and **T45 — Lindsey H., MM Facebook Member.** Both name the same two-part payload — tools + a definition of the target set *by the member*:

## 3. Techniques people credit — what a person literally does

Recurrence is counted across the sources fetched here only. "SMART IP" means the *specific tool and worksheet* belong to SMART; the underlying CBT/REBT mechanic does not, but **we must build our version from the mechanic and our own words**.

---

**K1 — Phone a person while you are already drinking, or before the first one.**
*What you do:* pick up the phone mid-slip and say what you have done, to a specific named person, before finishing.
*Verbatim:* "This time I went to the telephone (after I had taken about two drinks) and called L to tell her what I had done." (AA 1939, "A Feminine Victory"). LifeRing lists "LifeRing chats (especially for urgent drinking urges)" as a member tool (lifering.org/recovery-toolbox/, contributed by Anonymous).
*Recurrence:* AA 1939 ×1 explicit, LifeRing ×2 (chat-for-urges; "Phones – Get plenty of numbers of other recovering people and don't be afraid to use them" — Larry B.).
*Design note:* the reported blocker is **shame, not access**. She says the change was that she *no longer wanted to hide it*. A product that adds a button without dissolving concealment ships the wrong half.

**K2 — Say the relapse plan out loud to a non-reactive listener.**
*What you do:* state the plan, in detail, calmly, to someone who will not react.
*Verbatim:* "I tell her quietly what I intend to do. She says nothing. She does not get excited. She maintains a perfect calm. When I am through speaking, the whole idea has become absurd." (AA 1939, "Our Southern Friend")
*Recurrence:* ×1, but exceptionally specified — the *listener's* behaviour is the active part.

**K3 — Be sent to help someone worse off, immediately, before you are ready.**
*What you do:* go to a named person and tell them your story. Nothing else is asked.
*Verbatim:* "the doctor sent me to see another alcoholic who was in the hospital. All the doctor asked me to do was tell my story." (AA 1939, "The Salesman"). "I go to see a man I had been asked to visit and tell him my story. I feel much better! I have forgotten about a drink." ("Our Southern Friend"). "my doctor friend sent me to tell another alcoholic what my experience had been" ("The European Drinker"). "I know that incomparable happiness that comes from helping some other fellow get straightened out" ("The Fearful One").
*Recurrence:* ×4 in 1939 AA alone; recurs as *convening/facilitating* in LifeRing (Sharon B., Rodney, Ted, Rick), SMART (Caitlin, Loretta), RD (Chance: "Service"), MM (volunteering).
*Design note:* the dose is **assigned by a third party** and happens *early*, while the person is still unstable. Not "give back when you're strong."

**K4 — Daily fixed-time reading/recitation ritual, one item, reviewed at night.**
*What you do:* WFS — get up fifteen minutes early, read the statements, pick one, use it consciously all day, review it before sleep. AA 1939 — read a passage every morning. RD — sit, then recite a fixed liturgy.
*Verbatim:* "arise each morning fifteen minutes earlier than usual and go over these statements. Then begin to think about each one by itself. Take one statement and use it consciously all day. At the end of the day, just before sleeping, review the use of it and what affects it had that day for you and your actions." (Jean Kirkpatrick, WFS). "Every morning I read a part of the Bible and ask God to carry me through the day safely." (AA 1939, "The Salesman"). Synyi's sit → Five Remembrances → metta → refuge (RD).
*Recurrence:* ×4 orgs (WFS, AA, RD, LifeRing "read daily as a 'daily do'").
*Design note:* the WFS version is the most portable — **one item per day, chosen in the morning, scored at night**. Doctrine-free.

**K5 — End-of-day debrief with one other person, against a fixed question set.**
*What you do:* phone or meet a friend nightly and answer five questions.
*Verbatim:* "Debriefing at the end of the day: I get together with a friend on the phone or in-person and we talk about how our day went. I try to answer the following questions: A) How did my feelings change throughout the day? B) What did I do for my sobriety? C) In what ways did I jeopardize my sobriety? D) What do I need to do tomorrow? E) What is something good about myself (different each day). I usually feel better about how the day went when I do this." (Mark C., lifering.org/recovery-toolbox/)
*Recurrence:* ×1 as a full protocol; the AA analogue is Mary M.'s "I was to share the emotional turmoil of each day with a trusted one of them."
*Design note:* the closest thing in the corpus to a directly implementable daily check-in schema.

**K6 — Maintain the channel when you don't need it, so it exists when you do.**
*What you do:* call one or two people daily just to say hi, deliberately when nothing is wrong.
*Verbatim:* "Because it can be awkward to ask for help when I really need it, I have found it useful to call one or two phone buddies on a regular basis (every day for me) just to say hi and check-in. When I really need them for the support I have already established a relationship and it comes naturally to mention any current dilemma" (Mark C., LifeRing).
*Recurrence:* ×1 explicit; strongly corroborated by Burk Jackson's SMART post on scaffolding removal at year three/seven/fifteen.
*Design note:* pre-warming the relationship is the whole trick. Crisis-only contact fails because *asking* is the hard part.

**K7 — Somebody physically comes and gets you.**
*What you do:* nothing. Another member arrives and drives.
*Verbatim:* "a woman showed up at my door within an hour and said she was going to drive me to my first meeting." (Mary M., AA Agnostica). Grapevine's "Get in the Car" standfirst: "He was four months sober, fresh out of jail and trying to avoid his sponsor. Good try!"
*Recurrence:* ×2.
*Design note:* the transition from *intent* to *first attendance* is handled by a human, within an hour, with a vehicle. Software cannot do this; it can only trigger it.

**K8 — Co-write a concrete, ordered life-repair list with the group.**
*What you do:* the group sits with you after the meeting and builds a task list; you execute one item at a time.
*Verbatim:* "Taking me out after meetings for coffee and stringing the pieces of my life together. Making lists. Top of the list was get a job. Then talk to the mortgage company. Talk to the kids. Get a haircut. Clean myself up. A tiny step at a time." (Mary M.)
*Recurrence:* ×1, plus Tania M.'s solo version in the LifeRing toolbox: "Put the drink down and go and have a shower. Put on clean clothes and brush my hair nicely. Now I look acceptable enough to face the rest of my life….write myself a list of what I am going to be doing with it."
*Design note:* this is **not** the moral inventory. It is admin triage, done socially, in the hour after a meeting.

**K9 — Sponsorship as a role that can be temporary, fired, or emergency-assigned.**
*What you do:* ask one person; treat the arrangement as revisable.
*Verbatim:* "I didn't know you were allowed to fire a sponsor. I thought once you picked one, you were locked in; that resistance meant ego, and ego meant danger." (M.G., Grapevine). "Fresh out of treatment, he desperately asked for a sponsor. One guy stepped up, but only for a while." (Mike W.). "What started as an emergency fix for a five-alarm situation became an incredible 28-year relationship" (Betsy S.). RD's equivalent role is "mentor/wise friend"; Chance: "I regularly meet with both a mentor and a Buddhist teacher."
*Recurrence:* ×4 (AA Grapevine ×3, RD ×1), plus Mary M. sponsoring across belief lines.
*Design note:* the *published* AA position tolerates temporary, replaceable and emergency sponsors. The *folk* position (M.G.'s "locked in") does not — and that gap is where the harm sits.

**K10 — Crosstalk: a meeting where people may respond to you.**
*What you do:* attend a format that permits direct reply and requested advice.
*Verbatim:* "I like LifeRing because its meetings encourage crosstalk, and thereby give recovering people the opportunity to experience normal group interaction soberly, and to practice social skills in a supportive environment." (David). "Respectful crosstalk is what I love about LifeRing & yearn for in my recovery." (Rick H.). "no one dictates to you, only compassionate advice and sharing of experiences" (Dan, BC). Formal rule: "Ordinarily, no one will offer to tell you what to do, or give you advice, unless you expressly ask for it." (lifering.org/recovery-toolbox/)
*Recurrence:* ×3 member accounts + 1 org rule. **Directly contradicts AA's no-crosstalk norm.**
*Design note:* consent-gated advice — advice on request only — is the specific configuration credited.

**K11 — Recurrence of the same faces on a schedule.**
*Verbatim:* "it helps to see the same faces every week, which tells me it works" (Sharon B.). "my weekly participation — now over 8 years — keeps me away from the ‘slippery slope.’" (Donna). "having a community that is built around and assumes sobriety" (Donna).
*Recurrence:* ×4+ across LifeRing; Mary M.'s daily-meeting mandate in AA; Chance's "Two nights per week, I met with Sangha".
*Design note:* several people name **attendance regularity and face-recurrence** as the ingredient — not the content of the meeting, and explicitly not the steps.

**K12 — Personify the urge as a separate agent and refuse it.**
*What you do:* name it, treat it as an outside voice, decline.
*Verbatim (member):* "Get to recognize “the beast”. Treat him as someone else who inhabits your brain, but cares nothing for you and will do or say anything to get high." (George, LifeRing). "Lloyd the Bartender (aka the Limbic Brain)" (Laura AKA Bones, LifeRing). "My personal name for the Beast or the LB or Lloyd the Bartender or whatever – “the drunk”. “So the drunk wants a drink? Too damn bad!”" (Jill T., LifeRing).
*Verbatim (org, SMART — **IP**):* "give them a name and treat them like an outside force trying to manipulate you" (smartrecovery.org/personify-and-disarm).
*Recurrence:* ×3 LifeRing members + SMART's formal tool (DISARM).
*Design note:* **members invented this independently and named it themselves.** We can ship urge-externalisation; we must not ship SMART's DISARM worksheet or acronym.

**K13 — Dispute the belief, not the urge (REBT / ABC).**
*What you do:* write the activating event, the belief, the consequence; dispute the belief; install a replacement.
*Verbatim (member):* "years of reading Albert Ellis and other writers on CBT/REBT which made perfect sense but which I could never seem to put into practice finally “gelled”." … "I am responsible for my own feelings – it is what I tell myself about other people's actions that upsets me, not the actions in and of themselves." (Jill T., LifeRing)
*Verbatim (org, SMART — **IP**):* "By looking at the Activating event, our Beliefs about it, and the Consequences… When we add a D (Dispute your beliefs) and E (Effective new Belief)" (smartrecovery.org/abcs).
*Recurrence:* ×1 member + SMART's core tool. Note Jill's timeline: **years of correct understanding before it became usable.** Comprehension ≠ capability.

**K14 — Cost-benefit analysis / hierarchy of values.**
*What you do:* write out benefits and costs of using vs not using; rank what you actually value.
*Verbatim (member):* "acronyms like Hierarchy of Values (HOV) and Cost Benefit Analysis (CBA) are dropped on the daily in my house" (ChrisTLynne, SMART).
*Status:* **SMART IP** — the CBA worksheet must be rebuilt, not copied. Only one first-person recurrence found, and it is a *mention*, not a description of use. Weakest-evidenced of the named SMART tools in this corpus.

**K15 — Substitute an absorbing activity (SMART: VACI; LifeRing: many names).**
*Verbatim:* "baking is one of my favorite Vital Absorbing Creative Interest (VACIs)" (ChrisTLynne, SMART — **VACI is SMART IP**). "The very best thing I have ever done to keep the urges at bay is to move rocks. This may seem like a joke, but it truly works. Not only does it keep you busy, but physical labor leaves me with no strength to want to drink. And not just rocks, but also organizing closets or cleaning out the fridge." (Denise S., LifeRing). "I joined a lot of tennis leagues and started playing duplicate bridge." (Leslie JO). "Playing music again has become a large part of Caitlin's recovery." (SMART).
*Recurrence:* ×5+ across 3 orgs. **One of the best-attested techniques in the corpus**, and it is doctrine-free.

**K16 — Engineer the physical environment and the route.**
*What you do:* remove all supply; change the route home; put a physical obstacle between hand and shelf; post reminders.
*Verbatim:* "If you drink at home after work, DON'T GO HOME! Go to the gym, or the library, or the movies… If you MUST go home, because of pets or children, take a different route." (Laura AKA Bones, LifeRing). "Get every single bit of drug and/or alcohol-related items out of your house. Leave nothing; nothing for company, no cooking wines or sherry, nothing whatsoever." (same). "If I have to go into the grocery store for regular shopping, I put on those gloves because it's impossible to reach for the liquor shelf without noticing them as a reminder of my commitment to sobriety." (Sally, LifeRing). "I have “reminders” posted around my apartment… It would be impossible to be inside my home for more than twenty seconds without knowing that I am an alcoholic in recovery." (Mark P., LifeRing).
*Recurrence:* ×4 LifeRing + Mary Bradford's "break the five o'clock habit" + Craig M.'s "Stay away from parties in early sobriety."
*Design note:* the white-gloves trick is the most elegant thing in the corpus — an object that makes the automatic reach *noticeable* rather than *forbidden*.

**K17 — Kill unstructured time by pre-planning days off.**
*Verbatim:* "Lose the unstructured free time! Make a plan. Don't find yourself sitting around wondering what to do. If you do, your addiction will tell you what to do. Plan for your days off, at least three or four days in advance. Write it down, and then follow through." (Laura AKA Bones, LifeRing). "Most important in early sobriety to me was to PLAN ahead in situations that might be a problem. I always had an exit plan if I was at a drinking event" (Leslie JO). "Taking a non-drinking support person with me to activities." (Anonymous, LifeRing).
*Recurrence:* ×3.

**K18 — Carry a written artefact of the worst state.**
*Verbatim:* "One thing that helped me a lot when I got sober was to actually write down how drinking made me feel, and all the guilt, shame, the behaviors I exhibited, etc. Just having that small, folded piece of paper in my wallet helped a lot… I rarely even looked at it, because I knew what was written there." (Laura L., LifeRing). "Take a snapshot of yourself at your worst. Refer to it when you are tempted to have a drink." (George). "Remember your hangovers, arrests, and drug overdoses. Keep these memories fresh and do not allow yourself to romanticize the “good old days”." (Craig M.). "I ask it if it would like to see some old film clips of what happens when I drink… I set up the mental film projector and play as much as I can take from my archives." (MOG).
*Recurrence:* ×4, all LifeRing. Note Laura L.'s finding: **the artefact worked without being read.** Possession, not consultation.

**K19 — Announce non-drinking to a new person at each event.**
*Verbatim:* "I also planned and did meet someone new at these events who I would tell I did not drink. That way if I thought about drinking there was a new friend who would be surprised if I did." (Leslie JO, LifeRing). "It is much harder to deceive your friends and loved ones and relapse after they have learned the true extent of your substance abuse." (Larry B.).
*Recurrence:* ×2. A manufactured, on-the-spot accountability audience.

**K20 — Moderation-specific: scheduled abstinent days, "one less / one later / one water", tolerance reset, and a single in-head rule.**
*What you do:* fixed alcohol-free days per week (negotiable but tracked); a 30-day AF block to reset tolerance; NA beer as a substitute; one memorised bright-line rule for high-risk settings.
*Verbatim:* "I did 30 days AF… With the use of non-alcoholic beer, taking 3-4 days AF per week, I was able to bring down my tolerance to the point that 4 beers were more potent than 8 beers were previously." … "The phrase 'no shots' is in my head" (Brian, MM). "I like the accountability and the ability to be honest when we juggle the ABS days around." (Steph F., MM).
*Recurrence:* ×2 explicit member accounts.
*Design note:* Brian also pre-committed to **naltrexone as a fallback if the behavioural plan failed** — a decision rule set *before* the test, which is rare and good practice. ⚠ Everything in K20 is **incompatible with every other org here**, all of which are abstinence-based.

**K21 — Treat the whole programme as a series of experiments you own.**
*Verbatim:* "Your personal recovery program is a series of experiments. You'll try different ideas and behaviors, and if they help you stay sober, you'll keep them. If they lead to a relapse, you're encouraged to learn from the experience and try something new." … "a relapse may be a key part of your learning experience. “A fall into the pit, a gain in your wit.” If you have a relapse, feel free to share the experience with your group promptly, and pick up where you left off." (lifering.org/recovery-toolbox/). "I cannot tell anyone else how to get sober. I can only tell you what worked to get ME sober." (Jill T.). "we have no 'hard and fast rules' as there simply is no such thing as a 'program' that works for everyone." (Jill T.).
*Recurrence:* the LifeRing house philosophy + Caitlin's SMART account of relapse without reset.
*Design note:* **no clock reset** is the operative detail. Pick up where you left off.

**K22 — Service / convening as a sobriety technique, not altruism.**
*Verbatim:* "I've been a convenor in No Cali for almost a year now and it is one of the best service position I've had." (Sharon B.). "I went from a person looking for help, to a convenor and a woman with a message of hope" (Bettye D.). "Long term, my most powerful personal sobriety tool has been the commitment to LifeRing and the meetings – no way could drinking ever substitute for the personal satisfaction I get from my involvement." (Jill T.). "I volunteered to do an online support group as soon as I qualified so I could start to give back to others." (Leslie JO). "I take on service positions in my Recovery Dharma groups" (Chance). Caitlin drives a 142-mile round trip to facilitate.
*Recurrence:* ×6+ across AA, LifeRing, SMART, RD. **The single most cross-organisational technique in the corpus** — it survives every doctrinal disagreement.

## 4. Backfire and harm accounts

**B1 — Meditation used as an escape hatch (Recovery Dharma, from inside the practice).**
> "I completely misused meditation when I first started practice. Instead of using meditation as a method for meeting reality, I used it as an escape. In this way, meditation served as a substitute for the drugs I no longer had easy access to." — Chance, *Recovery Dharma* 2nd ed., p. 74.
The fix he reports is not more meditation but **teachers and other people**: "I finally had fellows on the path, and I finally had teachers. Both were absolutely necessary to the flourishing of my meditation and Dharma practice."

**B2 — Meetings that lower self-worth.**
> "Like so many others, the 12 step approach did not fit me at all! I became very depressed and my self-esteem diminished after each meeting." — Connie, Moraga CA (LifeRing)
Corroborated at the level of org design by WFS, which exists because its founder judged a fault-admission model wrong for women already carrying shame; and by Gary P.: "I think therefore I am … sick. That used to be my AA schema of the world."

**B3 — Step One as an absolute blocker, costing decades.**
> "I never could go to an AA meeting, even the first step could not be taken. After 25 years of hard drinking and many attempts at quitting…" — Gil, Benicia CA (LifeRing)
Not "the steps were unhelpful" — **the first sentence of the programme was un-sayable, so no help was received at all.** The most consequential harm mechanism in the corpus.

**B4 — Powerlessness as retraumatising language.**
> "As a survivor of trauma among so many other survivors of trauma, I know that powerlessness is not what I want my recovery to be based on." — Amy, *Recovery Dharma*
This is a founding-member's stated design rationale, not a passing remark: "When we started Recovery Dharma, one of our core intentions was to emphasize that this is a program of empowerment, not powerlessness."

**B5 — Sponsorship experienced as control, with no exit understood.**
> "when I started to feel confused and controlled in the sponsor relationship, I assumed the problem was me." — M.G., Los Angeles (AA Grapevine, Sept 2026)
The harm here is **a false belief about the rules** ("I thought once you picked one, you were locked in"), not the rules themselves. Any one-to-one pairing feature we build must state the exit clause on day one.

**B6 — Support that does not transfer between locations.**
Amy (RD) had five years and a functioning programme, moved states, and the same programme in a different subculture did not hold: "The old-timers sounded like cult leaders. The repetition of readings and sayings and slogans began to wear thin." Her marriage and recovery both destabilised. **What she had was a room, not a method** — and the room was not portable.

**B7 — Scaffolding removal in long-term recovery.**
> "somewhere around year three, or year seven, or year fifteen, the scaffolding quietly comes down. People assume you're fine. You assume you're supposed to be fine." … "Sometimes you've spent so long being the strong one… that you genuinely forget you're allowed to need something too." — Burk Jackson, 31 years (SMART blog, Jun 2026). ⚠ He is promoting his own platform in the same post.

**B8 — Institutional exclusion for changing the wording.**
Three Toronto agnostic AA groups were delisted by their intergroup. Reported by AA Agnostica: *"Widening Our Gateway… was officially “suspended from any involvement at Toronto Intergroup” by a vote of 27 to 17."* Member comment from another region: *"It's too bad the Toronto (and Boston, by the way) intergroups are so ignorant, rigid, and untraditional that they would practice this despicable discrimination, and are callously indifferent to the fates of alcoholics who want to get sober and who can't stomach the God stuff."* — Pat N., 32 years sober.

**B9 — Insight without behaviour change, for years.**
"The Doctor's Nightmare" (AA 1939): "I gave the matter much time and study for the next two and a half years, but still got tight every night nevertheless." Jill T. (LifeRing) on REBT: "which made perfect sense but which I could never seem to put into practice." Two independent traditions, same failure mode.

**B10 — The first meeting simply not landing.**
"The Salesman" (AA 1939) attended, felt "quite outside of the circle", went home and resumed. He returned **six months later** and it took. A product that treats a single non-conversion as a failed acquisition is modelling this wrong.

All the doctor asked me to do was tell my story." Neither host is an official AA publication; the agreement of two independent transcriptions is the basis for confidence, not either host's authority.
